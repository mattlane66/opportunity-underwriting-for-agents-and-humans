# Demand Evidence

## Principle

A gap is not a market. Demand evidence should be graded by how close it is to actual economic behavior.

| Tier | Evidence | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | purchases, contracts, paid pilots, deposits, switching, budget reallocation | strongest direct market evidence |
| B | substitute spend, consultants, internal labor, workaround stacks, measurable problem cost | demonstrates economic sacrifice |
| C | repeated usage, retention, procurement, internal-build attempts, high-intensity workarounds | behavioral demand, not necessarily WTP at proposed price |
| D | choice-based conjoint, discrete choice, real pricing experiments, reservation-price studies | structured preference evidence when based on real respondents/behavior |
| E | surveys, interviews, stated purchase intent | useful but vulnerable to hypothetical bias |
| F | search, social discussion, traffic, press, community activity | attention/discovery signal only |

Tier E/F evidence must never be promoted into “market demonstrated” without stronger support.

## Questions to answer

1. What do customers do today?
2. What money, labor, risk, or opportunity cost do they incur?
3. What evidence shows that the problem is consequential rather than merely annoying?
4. What has caused customers to switch, procure, build internally, or abandon alternatives?
5. What evidence supports willingness to transfer money or budget at a realistic price?

## AI evidence limitation

Synthetic personas, AI role-play, generated survey respondents, or model-predicted preferences are not customer evidence. AI may analyze real evidence but does not become the sample.
